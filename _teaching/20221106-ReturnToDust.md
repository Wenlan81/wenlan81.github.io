A few days ago, to complete an assignment for an elective course, I watched a highly controversial film online, *Return to Dust*. After finishing it, my first impression was that this is a rare gem of a movie. In recent years, Chinese cinema has struggled to return to its former state of development. In fact, it has become quite limited, even risk-averse. Few are willing to venture into uncharted territory; instead, they repeatedly recycle frameworks and cores of previously successful films, producing "formulaic movies" that offer nothing new. *Return to Dust* has indeed broken this mold, though it hasn’t entirely shifted the status quo or opened up new directions for Chinese cinema. Nevertheless, its emergence symbolizes something positive. In this post, I’d like to briefly share my interpretation of the film.

01 Animals
======
Two animals play significant roles in the film: a donkey and a swallow. I believe they symbolize different meanings.

Let’s start with the donkey, as it’s the first living creature to appear in the movie. The donkey owned by Ma Youtie is a consistent presence throughout the story. At the very beginning, during the title sequence, there’s an off-screen voice of a woman calling for “Lao Si” (Ma Youtie), but instead of Ma Youtie, the camera shows the donkey poking its head through a window. I think this choice by the director is meant to overlap the images of Ma Youtie and the donkey, implying that, like the donkey, Ma Youtie is driven and used by others without regard for his own feelings.

Another significant moment involving the donkey is toward the end, when Ma Youtie sets it free. He unties all the ropes and lets it go, but the donkey doesn’t move. Frustrated, Ma Youtie calls it “spineless.” Here, Ma Youtie is indirectly scolding himself — he spent his entire life working for others, never resisting or standing up for himself. Even when given the chance to leave the countryside for a better life in the city, he lacked the courage and chose to stay in the village and farm. Once again, the director connects the donkey to Ma Youtie, highlighting his tragic plight.

The second significant animal is the swallow, whose presence frequently coincides with that of the couple. Wherever the couple goes, the swallow appears as well. Early on, when their first home is demolished, Ma Youtie retrieves a fallen swallow’s nest from the rubble. Though the nest is empty, he remarks that the swallows will struggle to find their home when they return. Just like the couple, the swallows’ home is reduced to ruins by the bulldozers. When the couple moves to a new home, they find another swallow’s nest there, and after they build their own house, they bring the nest along. Even during the demolition of their second home, Ma Youtie takes the swallow’s nest with him. I believe this symbolizes two things: first, the swallow’s repeated displacement mirrors the couple’s forced relocations due to rural development projects; second, the swallow represents the kind of life Ma Youtie longs for—a life where, no matter how far one travels in winter, there’s always a home waiting in spring. Because Ma Youtie has lost his own home, he doesn’t want the swallows to face the same fate, which is why he protects the nest time and again.


02 Rhythm
======
The film’s pacing is extremely slow and avoids strong emotional highs. In Chinese culture, traditionally significant events such as weddings and funerals are portrayed with minimal detail in this film. For instance, the wedding between Ma Youtie and Cao Guiying is only briefly shown. This is understandable, as their marriage was arranged under duress and isn’t something worth celebrating. However, even during the climax of the film—Cao Guiying’s death—the director conveys her drowning in just a two-second shot of Ma Youtie holding her and crying. The rest is represented by a single black-and-white photo on the wall. This avoidance of overt emotionality paradoxically makes the story even more heartbreaking, sending a message: people like Cao Guiying, even in death, are deemed insignificant. Their lives are like a mundane diary, filled with joys and sorrows that go unnoticed because they cause no ripples.

In addition to its slow pacing, the film’s dialogue is handled differently from most films that use the 180-degree rule, which alternates between close-ups of two characters speaking. Instead, *Return to Dust* often keeps both characters in the same frame, capturing them conversing directly. This technique lends the film a documentary-like realism and aligns with the director’s intent to prevent the audience from fully identifying with Ma Youtie’s perspective. By avoiding emotional extremes, the director aims to simply narrate the couple’s tragic story calmly. As spectators rather than participants, the audience better grasps the injustices they face. The film isn’t just a lament about a harsh world; it subtly calls for warmth and humanity, urging viewers to extend kindness to others instead of remaining indifferent like the cold, detached characters in the story.


03 Love
======
While *Return to Dust* is a profoundly sad film, one of its central themes is “love.” It’s hard to define whether Ma Youtie and Cao Guiying’s relationship qualifies as “romantic love,” but it undoubtedly involves a broader sense of love. These two marginalized individuals illuminate each other’s paths, forging a radiant bond worth celebrating. I often say that love is the closest thing to magic in this world—it has the power to transform anyone as long as it’s strong enough.

Some question the film’s ending, arguing that it’s implausible for Ma Youtie to be so devastated by his wife’s death that he would take his own life, given that they were initially just two people forced together to survive. However, I see their relationship as more profound than mere partnership. Their lives became entwined, and losing that mutual support dealt a massive blow. Experiencing a happier life together made Ma Youtie unwilling to return to his solitary, monotonous existence. For him, life without Cao Guiying was unbearable. His decision to end his life reflects not an all-consuming romantic love but the inability to face a loveless world again. Love had made him vulnerable—a paradoxical aspect of love.

In one of the final scenes, an old man comforts Ma Youtie by saying, “It’s easier being alone.” Ma Youtie pauses, turns around as if to say something, but ultimately says nothing. This moment is open to interpretation. One possibility is that Ma Youtie wanted to refute the old man. If he agreed, he would have nodded immediately, rather than hesitating. He likely disagreed with the notion that life would be easier without Cao Guiying. However, his years of silence and restraint stopped him from voicing his thoughts. Another possibility is that he was considering saying goodbye to the villagers, knowing he wouldn’t stay, whether to follow his nephew to the city or end his life. Ultimately, he decided against it, realizing that his departure would be of little consequence to them. Regardless, this silent turn elevates his internal struggle, once again rooted in his bond with Cao Guiying—a story of love at its core.

Closing Thoughts
======
*Return to Dust* is a striking film. Despite its lack of emotional climaxes, it masterfully conveys a sense of sorrow that quietly seeps through the screen. Knowing the ending in advance didn’t make it any easier to witness the tragic fate of two kind and unfortunate people who had only just started to build a better life together.

The state of Chinese cinema has been suppressed for a long time due to various factors, including an uninspired industry, economic pressures, and societal constraints. While no immediate solution exists, it’s a topic that deserves attention from all who care about Chinese cinema. The road ahead is long, but I hope *Return to Dust* can help break the current impasse.
