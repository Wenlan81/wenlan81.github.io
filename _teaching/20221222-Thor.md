Over Christmas, I finally watched *Thor: Love and Thunder*, a film I’d been wanting to see for some time but hadn’t had the chance to. As the first original Avengers member to have a fourth solo movie, I initially had high expectations for it. However, after watching, I was left with just one question for Marvel: what on earth is this? If not for the title *Thor: Love and Thunder*, I might have thought I was watching some over-the-top, brightly colored Mary Sue cartoon.

Jane Foster, absent throughout *Thor: Ragnarok*, suddenly reappears with terminal cancer, only to draw power from Mjolnir under a promise to Thor to protect it, leading to her eventual demise. The dignified warrior-king Valkyrie, along with the two Thors, delivers Mjolnir to the villain, losing a kidney in the process. In the end, Thor defeats the antagonist with the help of Jane and a group of kids imbued with godly powers. Yet, despite all this effort, he still stands by and watches as the injured antagonist slowly drags himself to Eternity, only for Thor to ultimately save the world by talking the villain into submission with a heartfelt speech. What is this movie even trying to do???

The last time I saw a plot like this was in *Wonder Woman 1984*. In both films, the significant other exists solely to catalyze the protagonist’s growth and elevate their sense of self-worth, ultimately serving as a tragic narrative device to emphasize the protagonist’s devotion and worldliness. In these stories, the hero fights valiantly, sacrifices their beloved, and still fails to stop the villain, only to save the day through the sheer power of “heartfelt expression.” It’s pure fairy tale material. Whenever a weak-willed antagonist gets easily swayed by the hero’s words, I can’t help but applaud Thanos for his professionalism. Thanos, despite his eventual defeat, stands apart with his unwavering belief in his goals, meticulous execution, and visionary insight. Whether his ideology was right or not is debatable, but at least it was reasonable. Eliminating half the population to address resource scarcity, while inhumane, was at least a coherent plan. His determination is truly admirable.

The exaggerated storytelling of *Thor: Love and Thunder* gives Thor a definite “shonen anime protagonist” vibe—a hot-blooded fool who never gives up and has an indescribable but highly effective ability to unite his team. The scene where he grants children divine power is laughably over-the-top but undeniably inspiring. Placing such exaggerated traits on a superhero is a double-edged sword. On the one hand, it heightens the protagonist’s aura; on the other, it can disrupt the film’s realism. A decade ago, audiences may have wanted nothing more than mindless, extravagant superhero spectacles. But now, they expect more than flashy effects and fight scenes—they want thoughtful storytelling. They don’t want the Dark Knight trilogy to remain the sole masterpiece in superhero cinema. Marvel has proven its ability to craft grand narratives in the past, especially during the Avengers series. But since Phase 4, Marvel has been losing its way, producing increasingly chaotic and unfocused content. Some may call it “experimenting with new ideas,” but to me, it just feels like a mess.

The theme of *Thor: Love and Thunder* is, once again, “love”—a topic that’s been done to death yet remains difficult to execute well. The film confines itself to a formulaic depiction of love: the villain is driven by love turned to hatred, the heroes fight to protect love, someone sacrifices themselves, the protagonist delivers a tearful monologue about their bond with the deceased, and the villain is redeemed through love. Honestly, it feels like a middle-school essay template: introduce the theme, weave it throughout the story, and forcefully reiterate it in the conclusion. A decade ago, audiences might have accepted this formula, but after countless superhero movies, it’s no longer enough to satisfy viewers. It’s inevitable that such tropes now fall flat.

Marvel was once brilliant at portraying love. Captain America and Iron Man are prime examples of how Marvel could elevate a simple concept into something profound and moving. The iconic moment when Iron Man snaps his fingers is etched into countless fans’ memories, often displayed as desktop backgrounds. This is why, as an idealist, I’ve always preferred Marvel over DC. While DC delves into the gritty realities of human nature, Marvel excels at creating utopian worlds of hope and aspiration.

Yet, in Phase 4 of the Marvel Cinematic Universe, the studio seems to have lost its touch. It has failed to deliver truly remarkable works, even faltering in its once-masterful handling of love as a theme. Instead, it piles on humor and spectacle, diluting the emotional depth that once defined its films. I never considered Marvel movies to be mere popcorn flicks; some of them resonated deeply, leaving audiences reflective and emotional long after the credits rolled. But now, it feels like Marvel is churning out content purely for popcorn appeal.
