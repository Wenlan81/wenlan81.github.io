Recently, as I’ve been grappling with uncertainty about my future career path—debating whether to pursue a cross-disciplinary route combining psychology and business—I decided to watch *The Wolf of Wall Street*, often touted as a seminal film for business students. To be honest, the film fully aligns with my preconceived stereotypes about the finance industry: chaotic, high-pressure, and driven by greed. These are precisely some of the reasons I hesitate to step into the world of business. I want to make money, but I don’t want to earn it in a way that goes against my principles. Yet, at times, I wonder: if I’m already wavering between my principles and the lure of wealth, does that mean I’ve already begun to compromise my values? 

While the movie may not have illuminated a clear path for my future, *The Wolf of Wall Street* is undeniably a powerful and impactful film. In this essay, I’d like to reflect on the movie from two perspectives: its artistic qualities as a film and the personal insights it offered me about my values and ambitions.


### The Film’s Perspective

Watching the movie was a shocking experience. While I had heard about *The Wolf of Wall Street*’s bold and unrestrained approach, the barrage of profanity, obscene gestures, and even nudity felt like a slap in the face for someone accustomed to the more restrained world of Chinese cinema. If you were to call this an artistic masterpiece with profound ideas, I’d have to disagree based on my own values. However, as a film, I deeply admire its technical sophistication and overall quality. Its filming techniques effectively immerse the audience, making them feel the protagonist’s emotional highs and lows as if they were experiencing them firsthand. From a cinematic standpoint, the success of *The Wolf of Wall Street* is beyond question.

That said, I initially misinterpreted the film’s purpose. I started watching it with the hope that it might serve as a source of inspiration or enlightenment for my future career in business. However, halfway through, I realized it’s fundamentally a crime comedy. But unlike Robin Hood-style stories that deliver satisfying, morally grounded thrills, this film is purely about greed—one man’s insatiable pursuit of wealth and pleasure by any means necessary.

As a crime film, *The Wolf of Wall Street* stands out. It doesn’t rely on special effects, action-packed fight scenes, or high-tech hacking sequences. The most intense physical conflict in the movie is probably Jordan and Donnie wrestling over a telephone. The film vividly demonstrates that language can be far more powerful than any other criminal method. Jordan doesn’t need explosives or hackers—just his words. He simply stands there, speaks, and money flows into his pockets. As a psychology student, I find this type of crime far more intriguing than physical confrontations. Negotiation and persuasion have always been areas of interest for me, although I’d never want to use these skills to deceive or exploit others. Instead, I’d like to employ them in ways that genuinely help the people I persuade, much like Jordan’s early aspirations when he spoke to his first mentor.

Though Jordan’s motivational speeches to his team and sales pitches to clients often resemble cult tactics, he never forces anyone at gunpoint to follow his lead. People willingly comply because Jordan knows precisely how to tap into their psychological pain points and desires, making himself appear credible and his proposals compelling. I admire his skills, but I wouldn’t want to become him.

### A Reflection on Values

By the end of the film, I couldn’t help but notice how the actors delivered top-tier performances. The chaotic and outrageous plot—filled with drug binges and wild antics—depicts situations far removed from the average person’s reality, yet everything feels disturbingly authentic. The scene where Jordan, high on Quaaludes, struggles to crawl back to his car and then home to fight Donnie is an absolute standout. It’s hard to believe such an explosive performance came from a sober actor. From physical control to facial expressions to dialogue delivery, Leonardo DiCaprio was phenomenal. It’s truly a pity this role didn’t win him an Oscar.

From another perspective, *The Wolf of Wall Street* didn’t provide the career inspiration I initially hoped for. Instead, it had the opposite effect, amplifying every aspect of business that deters me: the overwhelming pressure, the relentless pursuit of profit, and so on. However, I also found myself instinctively defending the industry I claimed to dislike. When the movie depicted madness, I told myself it must be exaggerated for dramatic effect. When it showed ruthless behavior, I wondered if not everyone in the industry was that bad. Deep down, I couldn’t ignore the desire for wealth buried beneath my insistence on principles.

I’ve repeated the word “principles” throughout this essay, but truthfully, I’m not even sure what my core principles are. Is it psychology? Perhaps, but my interest is limited to specific aspects of the field. I enjoy studying people—their thoughts, behaviors, and desires—but I have little interest in delving into other psychological subfields. In some ways, Jordan embodies qualities I aspire to have: the ability to stand before someone, have a conversation, and predict their actions. This skill fascinates me, gives me a sense of accomplishment, and makes me feel in control. At the same time, I don’t want to explain how I arrived at my predictions, as it would strip away my sense of mystery and security. This contradiction makes me feel like I’d make a good villain—analyzing people, understanding them, and then manipulating them with a touch of mischief. Sometimes, I even fantasize about having superpowers and wonder if I’d become a world-destroying villain or a hero who saves the world and disappears into the sunset, leaving only a mysterious silhouette.

Ultimately, my desire to study behavior and desire is influenced by another principle: the hope to be a kind person. I want to live without regrets, avoiding malicious speculation while also setting boundaries. These principles, while significant to my interests and values, are not concrete enough to shape my life’s direction. Combined with my desire for wealth, they’ve left me at an impasse. I can’t fully commit to either path, and I can only hope that, someday soon, I’ll discover a passion that gives me clarity and purpose.

Returning to the movie, Jordan may not seem like someone with strong convictions, but in reality, he has consistently adhered to one idea: making money. From the moment he set foot on Wall Street, “making money” has been his dream and driving force. Not even Black Monday or the FBI could stop him. Even after his release from prison, he repeated the same line he used when he first started: “Sell me this pen.” Call it obsession or unwavering determination, but Jordan certainly pursued his goal relentlessly. This taught me another lesson: no matter the field, as long as you have talent and passion and commit to it wholeheartedly, you can achieve success. That’s why finding a career you’re willing to dedicate your life to is so important. But that brings me back to square one—my uncertainty about my future. I know what interests me, but I struggle to figure out where my interests fit in the larger scheme of things. Analyzing individual behavior and desires seems to intersect with both business and psychology. If I follow this path, perhaps I’ll figure things out as I go.

Though *The Wolf of Wall Street* didn’t offer new ideas for my career, reflecting on the film helped me make some choices. Perhaps using my psychological expertise in the business world could be a path worth exploring.

In conclusion, *The Wolf of Wall Street* is a groundbreaking piece of cinema, offering a unique take on crime films. The director’s focus seems to lean more on crafting vivid characters and delivering visual impact than delving into profound themes. However, its exaggerated style and storyline compel viewers to confront their deepest desires. While the film didn’t help me embrace my inner desires, it did teach me to accept them and coexist with them.
